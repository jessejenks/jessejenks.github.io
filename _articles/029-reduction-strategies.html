Lambda Calculus Reduction Strategies

The Church-Rosser theorem says that the pure lambda calculus is a confluent system. This means that if a term has a normal form (it can't be reduced further), it is unique, and it doesn't matter what order you reduce terms in.

But there are some very useful terms which don't have a normal form, and there are practical reasons why reducing some part first is better than another. There are also times where we don't want everything to reduce to a “\(\beta\)-normal” or “\(\beta\eta\)-normal” form, and instead want to reduce to other kinds of normal forms.

A strategy for picking what order to reduce terms is called a reduction strategy. This is analogous to an order of operations for the lambda calculus.

Two common strategies are call-by-name (CBN) and call-by-value (CBV). These are both so-called “weak” strategies, which means they never reduce inside of an abstraction (and thus neither reduce to \(\beta\)-normal form). Instead, CBV reduces to something called weak normal form and CBN reduces to weak head normal form.

The practical difference between these strategies is this: CBV reduces arguments before applying to a function, while CBN does not.

There is a real tradeoff here. Imagine the following function foo(x, y) = x + y + y and the function call foo(1, veryExpensiveCalculation). With a CBN strategy, every occurrence of y means a re-evaluation of veryExpensiveCalculation. So clearly CBV is better, right?

But now consider foo(x, y) = x + x and the call foo(1, crashesIfEvaluated). With a CBV strategy, even though the parameter y is completely unused, this will always crash, since we always evaluate arguments first!

Both of these scenarios can and do occur, but in most programs, the latter case is rare while the former is common, {% include footnotes/link id="call-by-need" content="so most languages use a CBV-like strategy" %}.

Church-Rosser


    

        I emphasized a corollary of the Church-Rosser theorem earlier, that when a term has a normal form, it is unique. But one thing that can sometimes be confusing about the Church-Rosser theorem is that it applies even to all terms, even when they do not have a normal form.
    


    

        For example, writing \(I = \lambda z.z\) and \(\omega = \lambda x. x x\), there are two possible reductions of \((I \omega) (I \omega)\). But either choice is guaranteed to eventually reduce to the same term. In this case to \(\Omega = (\lambda x.x x)(\lambda x.x x)\), which is not normal and always reduces to itself.
